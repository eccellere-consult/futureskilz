## Instructional design
- **learning_need:** A fix that does not last wastes time and trust. People often treat what they can see, so the problem returns.
- **learning_objective:** Trace a repeating problem, step by step, from what you can see to a cause you can fix, using five checkable whys.
- **success_criteria:**
  - Writes the problem as one factual sentence.
  - Gives each answer as something that can be checked, and each answer explains the one before it.
  - Stops at a cause in the process that the team can change, not at a person's name or a guess.
  - Proposes one action aimed at that cause.
- **case_context:** A small packing line has shipped orders for one customer a day late for three weeks. Reminding the packers to hurry worked for two days, then the orders were late again. (Illustrative: written for this lesson.)
- **key_concept:** A symptom is what you notice. A root cause is the reason that, once removed, stops the problem from coming back. The five whys is the habit of asking why again and again, answering each time with something checkable.
- **method_or_framework:** The five whys: state the problem in one factual sentence, ask why, answer with a checkable fact, check it, repeat until you reach a cause the process lets you change, then test whether removing it would stop the problem.
- **step_by_step_application:**
  1. Problem: orders for one customer ship a day late.
  2. Why 1: the order was not packed before the truck arrived, because the packers were waiting for labels.
  3. Why 2: the labels were late, because label printing starts only after the final quantity is confirmed in the system.
  4. Why 3: the quantity was confirmed late, because the last batch of the day is booked into stock the next morning.
  5. Why 4: the last batch is booked the next morning, because the stores clerk's shift ends before the last batch finishes.
  6. Why 5: nobody books it before they leave, because there is no end-of-shift handover step for the last batch.
  7. Root cause: the process has no handover step. Action: add a five-minute end-of-shift handover and book the last batch before the shift ends.
- **worked_example:** The packing line case, walked through five whys. The answer 'the packers were slow' is rejected at step one because it explains nothing that can be checked or changed; the label timestamps can be.
- **common_mistakes:**
  - Stopping at a person ('careless', 'forgot') instead of asking what in the process allowed it.
  - Jumping to a solution before the cause is checked.
  - Answering with opinions or guesses instead of facts that can be checked.
  - Following only one chain when the problem has more than one cause.
  - Treating 'five' as a rule: stop when you reach a cause you can fix, not when you have counted five.
- **limitations_and_caveats:**
  - Works best on problems with a fairly direct chain of causes. Complex problems with several interacting causes need data and other tools alongside it.
  - The answers are only as good as the evidence behind them: check each one before acting.
  - It finds a cause you can act on, not necessarily the only cause.
- **learner_challenge:** A different problem: delivery notes keep showing wrong quantities. The first answer is 'the picker counted wrong.' Choose the best next why.
- **correct_response_and_rationale:** A good next why explains the previous answer with something you can check, and it points at the process, not at the person.
- **workplace_transfer_task:** Pick one problem from the last month that came back after a fix. On one card, write the problem as a factual sentence and your chain of whys. Mark which answers you have checked. Propose one action aimed at the deepest cause, and share it with your team lead.
- **summary_takeaways:**
  - Ask why until you reach a cause the process lets you change.
  - Every answer must be a fact you can check, and must explain the one before it.
  - Treat a person-level answer such as 'human error' as the start of the next question, not the end.
- **next_learning_recommendation:**
  - Try the 5 Whys tool on a real problem (/problem-solving/five-whys): Apply the method to a problem of your own, with checks.
  - Find which problem to solve first with a Pareto chart (/problem-solving/pareto): When there are many problems, the data shows which few matter most.
  - Show causes side by side with a fishbone (/problem-solving/fishbone): When one chain is not enough, a fishbone lays out several possible causes.
